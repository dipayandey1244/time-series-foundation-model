The story between observations.

A time-series model is asked to continue what it has seen. A world model asks what unseen process produced those observations. This is one story told from both sides of the measurement.

Begin with one changing signal. Follow how researchers turn its history into a forecast, then ask what the forecast can tell us about a world only partly observed.

01

Start with the trace, not the model.

One signal. Two readings. Adjust what the observer gets to see.

Imagine a machine whose internal temperature and load change over time. We do not watch every part of the machine continuously. We receive a sequence of meter readings: some are noisy, some are missing, and all arrive from a particular sensor. The model receives evidence, not the whole machine.

02

The papers ask how to forecast from traces.

Different mechanisms answer the same practical question: what can be reused across histories?

The observed sequence is a modeling object. A paper chooses how to represent it, what objective to learn, and what transfer evidence would count. Across the field, the change is not “statistics replaced by one giant model.” It is a widening set of ways to share structure across series.

2023

TimesFM · a decoder reads numeric patches

Short blocks of values become tokens in a causal Transformer. The model predicts a larger future patch, then rolls forward autoregressively. The paper reports zero-shot tests on held-out datasets using a 200M-parameter time-series model.

Can one pretrained model continue a new history without dataset-specific fitting?

2024

Chronos · forecast through a value vocabulary

Scale and quantize observations into discrete bins, then train a sequence model with a next-token objective. Sampling tokens gives a distribution over possible numeric futures after decoding.

Can the language-model recipe work when numbers are deliberately made into symbols?

2024

Moirai · handle varied series in one forecaster

Variable patch sizes, masks and distributional outputs address differences in sampling frequency, channels and value shape. LOTSA supplies broad pretraining data; zero-shot evaluation asks whether that scope transfers.

Can one model accommodate different frequencies, variables and distributions?

2024

UniTS · share a model across task families

Task prompts and shared weights connect forecasting with classification, imputation and anomaly detection. The question expands from many datasets to multiple kinds of time-series work.

How much task-specific machinery can a common model replace?

2024—25

Survey + TSFM-Bench · define the map and the test

The survey organizes design choices; TSFM-Bench standardizes data, model and evaluator dimensions. Together they remind us that a score is meaningful only alongside its task, split, horizon and adaptation protocol.

What exactly was compared, and what can the evidence support?

The turn in the storyA forecast can be excellent at continuing a pattern and still tell us little about why that pattern exists. To ask why, we need to distinguish the hidden process from the sensor trace.
03

The world is larger than its measurements.

The World Model Lab starts where a time-series table ends: with a process that is only partially visible.

Return to the machine. Its internal load, temperature, wear and control state evolve together, but a meter may report only one noisy temperature reading each minute. Two different internal conditions could produce similar readings. A model cannot infer what the sensor never reveals; it can only combine the evidence over time and make its uncertainty explicit.

1 · State evolves

A compact dynamical description says that the next hidden state depends on the current state, known inputs and unresolved disturbances.

xt+1 = f(xt, ut) + wt

2 · Sensors reveal a projection

What we measure is produced by an observation rule. Sensor noise and incomplete coverage limit what can be identified.

yt = h(xt) + εt
Important boundary: the representation learned by a forecasting model is not automatically the physical state. Calling it a useful “information state” is a predictive claim: it should preserve what matters for forecasting, not reconstruct every hidden variable.

Now switch the experiment above to Reveal the world. The red trajectory is the generating process. Blue dots are what the sensor actually delivered. Change coverage and noise: you will see the gap between a process and its observations. Where the two strands meetThe papers ask how a reusable model maps histories to futures. The world-model view asks whether those histories contain enough information to constrain the future in the first place.
04

History becomes a predictive state.

This is the bridge: compression is useful only when it retains evidence relevant to the future.

WorldA hidden system changes through time: xt+1 = f(xt, ut) + wt.
ObserveA sensor maps some of that state into measurements: yt = h(xt) + εt.
AccumulateThe model receives the available history Ht = (y1, …, yt), not the complete world.
RepresentA learned encoder turns history into zt = φθ(Ht).
PredictA useful zt supports a distribution over future observations.

The information-state idea

p(yt+1:t+K | Ht) ≈ p(yt+1:t+K | zt)

If this approximation is good, z compresses the history without discarding predictive information. It does not imply that z equals a true physical state. It is a property to investigate, not a guarantee granted by the model’s name.

That gives us a careful way to read the research. TimesFM, Chronos and Moirai propose different routes from numeric histories to forecasts. UniTS asks whether a shared representation can support different tasks. The survey maps the design space, while TSFM-Bench asks whether comparisons are being made on common ground. The world-model lens then adds a prior question: how informative were the observations that entered the model?
